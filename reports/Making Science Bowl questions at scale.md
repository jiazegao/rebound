# Generated Science Bowl rounds pass accuracy, fail originality

Rebound can mass-produce its own NSB-style practice rounds, and a blind pilot shows that getting the answers right is the easy part. One Claude model wrote 72 questions for one high school and one middle school round, and a second Claude model then solved them blind and checked them against sources: **none of the 72 had a wrong answer key, while 2 of the 36 real DOE questions mixed in as controls did**. The hard problems are originality, difficulty and freshness. **46% of first drafts asked for a DOE answer using DOE's own cue**, Rebound's duplicate check flagged none of them, and three more duplicates got past the writer's own clean-up. The high school round matched only DOE's easier early rounds, the middle school round fell short even of those, and **5 of the 12 Energy questions named a lab that DOE renamed in December 2025**. DOE's own process explains why Rebound needs its own supply. A contractor (ORISE, by its own account) writes several 17-round sets a season, at least two subject experts review each subject, and volunteers at 115 regional events plus an errata desk catch the rest. The packets are then destroyed and reappear as unlicensed PDFs only years later (about four for the newest sets), so **the 23,720 questions Rebound holds are everything DOE has published, the newest from 2022**. Legally, original questions fit DOE's own advice to "write your own", and no NSB rule mentions AI. The real limits are trademark and endorsement, never using unreleased official questions, and the fact that purely AI-written text has no copyright. The recommended approach is a pipeline that puts originality first: numbers computed in code, a blind solve by a second model, a screen for questions that reuse an existing answer and cue, and a human subject owner's sign-off. Its rounds would go out first as labeled team packets and be tested on real play before they enter the public library. At modeled API prices, **three fresh rounds a week cost roughly $110–$810 a year**, so reviewer time, not compute, sets the pace.

## DOE writes behind contractors and NDAs, then publishes years late

### A contractor drafts, two experts review, and the regionals catch the rest

DOE does not name its question writers; its sample-question pages, FAQ and volunteer pages credit no authors ([HS Sample Questions](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/HS-Sample-Questions); [NSB FAQ](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/FAQ)). The best current evidence is a May 2026 release from ORISE, the DOE contractor run by ORAU. It lists **"developing questions for the competition"** among its staff's work on the 36th National Science Bowl ([ORISE, May 2026, via search excerpt](https://orise.orau.gov/news/archive/2026/orise-supports-36th-annual-national-science-bowl.html)). Earlier records point the same way. A 2009 DOE FAQ said DOE uses government contracting to hire "a company" to write the regional and national questions ([archived FAQ, via search excerpt](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090514230543/http://www.scied.science.doe.gov/nsb/faq.htm)). In 2005, Avery Enterprises announced it was "the official writer" for both levels ([QBWiki mirror, via search excerpt](https://www.qbwiki.com/yahoo-group/14736.html)). An older, undated DOE Nevada fact sheet describes a different model, with questions "submitted by scientists at all DOE facilities as well as from other federal agencies and university consortia" ([DOE/NV fact sheet, via search excerpt](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130216171019/http://www.nv.doe.gov/library/factsheets/DOENV_1211.pdf)). The only official named in the review chain is National Coordinator Jan Tyler ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). Her name fills the Author field of most sample PDFs from 2011 to 2026, but that shows who exported the files, not who wrote the questions ([HS Set 17 Round 1](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/HS-Sample-Questions/Sample-Set-17/2022-HS-1.pdf)). Nothing public gives the writers' number, pay or selection. A search of USAspending found no separate NSB question-writing contract, which fits the work being paid for inside ORISE's main contract ([USAspending API](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/search/spending_by_award/)).

DOE describes its central review in one sentence: **"The questions in each subject area have been reviewed by at least two individuals with subject expertise to eliminate erroneous or ambiguous questions."** It uses multiple choice on purpose, to guard "against there being more than one correct answer" ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). The same manual admits that **"we continue to find errors, omitted words, and formatting issues almost every week."** It asks each regional to have its own experts review the packet before game day and to email any errors to the National Coordinator. She passes them to "the NSB subject-matter experts" and sends approved changes to every regional (same source). DOE documents no separate style edit, no formal fact-check against references, no playtesting with students and no statistics on how questions perform. A question's difficulty comes from its round, not from measurement: rounds 1–10 are "fairly equal in difficulty", questions get harder from round 11, and round 17 is the hardest ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). Content is "by and large sourced from science textbooks", from grade 6–8 texts for middle school regionals up to introductory college texts for the high school Finals ([Tips and Resources](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Tips-and-Resources)).

The real safety net is people. **More than 2,000 volunteers** worked the FY2025 competitions ([FY2027 WDTS budget narrative](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/budget/pdf/sc-budget-request-to-congress/2027/FY-2027-Request-Narrative---WDTS.pdf)). Each of the **115 regionals** reads its packet with moderators and question judges, and the manual asks each to keep subject experts on call at a "Science Bowl Central" ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). Even so, errors reach the published record. Rebound's importer has had to correct seven answer letters that DOE printed against the wrong option, and to flag typos such as "ANWER:" and a bonus printed under a second TOSS-UP heading ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md); [Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). Rebound has no crowd of live readers, so a generated set must catch its own errors before release.

### Rotating 17-round sets travel under NDA and are destroyed after play

DOE writes "multiple sets" each season. Each regional receives one in early January, "depending on the location and event date", to "limit the chances that a visitor might overhear a question" ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). A set has 17 rounds. Rounds 1–10 serve the round robin and the harder rounds 11–17 the elimination stage, and an in-person game ends when "23 toss-up questions have been read" (same source). The published sets show the mix changing. From 2014 to 2020 a round held 23 toss-up/bonus pairs, four per category except Energy, which had three. The 2021–22 virtual-era sets used 18 pairs, three per category, in a fixed Biology–Chemistry–Earth and Space–Energy–Math–Physics order ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)). DOE publishes no season total. One 17-round set of 23-pair rounds is 782 questions, so two sets per level means at least 3,128 regional questions a season. Adding Finals rounds, tiebreakers and spares gives roughly 3,500–5,000, an inference that fits a 2005 call for writers covering more than 4,000 questions ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md); [hsquizbowl, via search summary](https://www.hsquizbowl.org/forums/viewtopic.php?t=1268)). DOE also reuses material. Its 2022 middle school Round 7 contains the same 36 questions as its 2022 high school Round 10 ([Rebound MS import report](../scripts/doe/REPORT-MS.md)), and it warns that questions "could possibly be used in the future" ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)).

That reuse explains the security rules. Everyone who receives regional questions should complete a Non-Disclosure and Conflict of Interest Form. Moderators and judges must secure the material "whether it be on paper or computer-based" and return or delete it afterward. **"All regional questions must be collected and destroyed after the competition,"** and fewer than four questions may be aired for media use ([2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)). As recently as 2020, DOE required printed packets "due to the significant concerns surrounding unauthorized distribution of the NSB questions in electronic form" ([2020 manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/coordinator-resources/NSB_CoordManual.pdf?la=en&hash=A5A2CB04209FF8488FBF7412E7E22363D89CBAF6)). Rule 1-2 binds the players: "Under no circumstances should any players have access to any official National Science Bowl® questions at any time, other than those published on the NSB website" ([2027 Rules](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)). Corrections during play are tightly limited. Only the four players at the table may challenge, before the moderator reads the next question's category, and each team gets two unsuccessful challenges per round. Challenges go to subject experts at Science Bowl Central, and "All decisions of the judges are final." If two multiple-choice options are equally correct, either is accepted; if none is correct, the question is thrown out ([2027 Rules](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf); [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)).

### Releases now trail play by about four years, so Rebound already holds everything

The public gets past regional packets, one PDF per round. There are 17 high school sets from 2007–2022 (257 rounds) and 16 middle school sets plus four undated "Sample Rounds" (250 rounds), and nothing yet from the 2023–2026 seasons ([HS Sample Questions](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/HS-Sample-Questions); [MS Sample Questions](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/MS-Sample-Questions)). The PDFs' creation dates show the delay growing. The 2013–2019 sets appeared about 32–34 months after play, the 2020 and 2021 sets together in December 2024, and the 2022 sets in February 2026 ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md)). DOE's only caveat is that "as fields of science advance, the answers to some of the questions change," so the files are "a resource for sample questions, not sample answers" ([HS Sample Questions](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/HS-Sample-Questions)). A text search of all 509 sample PDFs found no copyright notice, license or usage restriction ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md)). In 2019 DOE described a bank that "grows by 32 rounds each year" ([Invitational Practice Tournament page](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Invitational-Practice-Tournament)); releases have slowed since.

For Rebound, the library is complete and closed. Its **23,720 questions average about 12 years old**, and none is newer than 2022. At two to four practice rounds a week, a team hears all 257 high school rounds in 64–128 weeks. In the worst case, four players drilling 30 minutes a day would leave Rebound's scrimmage builder with no fully fresh high school pair after about 30 weeks. Rounds balanced across the six categories run out sooner still, at about 160 high school and 100 middle school rounds, because high school Energy and middle school chemistry are the smallest pools ([Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). The community does publish fresh material, very roughly 10,000–15,000 invitational and school-team questions a year, and SB Reader alone collects about 102,000. But those questions "belong to their writers and tournaments" and cannot be imported without permission ([other producers analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/other_producers_and_tools.md); [SB Reader](https://github.com/sbreader/sbreader.github.io)). The same research found no tool that wrote original NSB-format questions as of November 2026. DOE is not about to fill the gap. Its FY2027 request raises the NSB budget line from $3.4 million to $6.1 million, but the increase pays for a national AI Challenge, not more releases ([FY2027 WDTS budget narrative](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/budget/pdf/sc-budget-request-to-congress/2027/FY-2027-Request-Narrative---WDTS.pdf)). The ORISE contract that covers question writing is being re-competed for October 2027, which could change who writes the questions ([ORISE-26 competition, via search excerpt](https://science.osti.gov/Acquisition-Management/ContractCompetitions/ORISE-26)).

## NSB house style is explicit enough to check by machine

The 2027 rules, dated 10-04-2026 and changed from 2026 only in their dates, set the limits a writer works within. They apply at both levels and in all six categories: Biology, Chemistry, Earth and Space Science, Energy, Mathematics and Physics ([2027 Rules](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)). Toss-ups score 4 and bonuses 10. Players have 5 seconds to buzz after a toss-up, and the captain has 20 seconds to begin a bonus answer. There are no calculators, only scratch paper, and no extra time for computation. Players may buzz once the category is read, and a wrong early buzz gives the other team 4 points and a full re-read. A multiple-choice answer counts if it is the letter or the choice text "exactly as indicated". Only "the best of the 4 choices" is acceptable, and players may not challenge a ruling on whether a spoken answer was exact. Appendix A sets the defaults: g = 9.8 m/s², sound at 340 m/s, angles in radians, and answers "in exact and simplest form" with no scientific notation unless asked. Chemical names or formulas must not be mistakable for another compound, and a person's last name alone is enough (same source). Each rule becomes a writing requirement. A toss-up must be fair at every word because players buzz early. Choices must be short and impossible to say two ways. Short answers need ACCEPT and DO NOT ACCEPT lines, because officials judge only against the printed line. And a bonus computation must fit 20 seconds of scratch work ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)).

DOE's guidance to writers runs to five "rules of thumb": short answers of one or two words "with minimal room for interpretation", as few words as possible, the conventions in the rules' appendix, questions that require "application of that knowledge" rather than bare recall, and an aim "not to stump students with esoteric facts" ([Tips and Resources](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Tips-and-Resources)). Neither DOE nor any invitational publishes a fuller style manual ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)). The house style can, however, be measured from DOE's newest packets. That turns style from taste into numbers a generator can be checked against:

| Feature (2019–22 DOE sets) | High school | Middle school |
|---|---|---|
| Median words in the question, toss-up / bonus | 20 / 25 | 18 / 22 |
| Share that is multiple choice (2022 sets alone) | 27.5% (39.6%) | 25.5% (39.2%) |
| Multiple-choice questions offering "All/None of the above" | 0 of 677 | 0 of 558 |

Source: measured across Rebound's copy of the DOE library ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)).

Other regularities in the same data are just as checkable. In the 2019–22 packets, 99.7% of high school multiple-choice questions end in a question mark. Every high school multiple-choice question has exactly four choices, each a median of two words long, and numeric choices are almost always in order. A capitalized NOT appears in only 1.5% of high school questions. Pronunciation guides in square brackets, with the stressed syllable in capitals ("vestibular [ves-TIB-u-lar]"), appear in 22%. Only about 14% of high school numeric questions spell out rounding or answer form; the rest rely on the appendix defaults. Three-quarters of high school Energy questions open with "Scientists at <named DOE lab> are studying…" and then ask an ordinary science question ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)). Middle school differs in content, not format. DOE pitches its middle school regionals at grade 6–8 science and Algebra II textbooks and its high school regionals at AP-level texts ([Tips and Resources](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Tips-and-Resources)), and middle school has used the high school category names since at least 2022 ([2027 Rules](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)). One caution applies to learning from old packets. A 2019 middle school answer line rejects "chordates", which Rule A-2(d) now accepts as "a synonymous common name". A generator should therefore learn its answer conventions from the rules, not from old answer lines ([NSB format analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_format_and_style.md)).

## The pilot produced flawless keys but recycled DOE's cues

The pilot, run on 2026-11-21, tested the idea end to end. A Claude model, working from its own knowledge without web access, wrote one high school round and one middle school round of 18 toss-up/bonus pairs each, following the category order and roughly the multiple-choice mix of DOE's 2022 Round 1 at each level. It reviewed its own questions, compared them with the library and replaced the ones that matched existing DOE questions. It then built a blind test for each level that mixed the 36 generated questions with 18 DOE questions, matched on category and on toss-up or bonus, and shuffled them under anonymous ids ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)). A separate session of a second Claude model for each level answered all 54 questions blind, rated their difficulty, flagged problems and guessed which were DOE's. Only then did it open the answer key, settle every disagreement and flag against sources, recompute every number in Python, check eight randomly chosen keys it had agreed with, and search the full library for duplicates ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The checker was a different model from the same family. Errors that both models share are the ones this test is least able to catch, a limit both checkers state.

| Outcome | HS generated (36) | HS DOE (18) | MS generated (36) | MS DOE (18) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Checker's blind answer matched the key | 36 | 16 | 36 | 18 |
| Key wrong after checking sources | **0** | **2** | **0** | 0 |
| Other problems that held up after checking | 1 giveaway, plus 3 outdated lab names the checker missed | 1 misspelled wrong choice | 9: giveaway or longest-choice clues, 2 outdated lab names, 3 duplicates of DOE questions | 3: an impossible given value, an item far above level, a defensible second answer |
| Matches to DOE questions in the final set (same answer and cue / same template, new numbers) | 0 / 9 | — | 3 / 4 | — |
| Checker's mean difficulty rating (1–5) | 1.94 | 2.50 (rounds 1–10: 1.91; rounds 11+: 3.43) | 1.94 | 2.61 (rounds 1–10: 2.33; rounds 11+: 3.17) |
| Checker guessed the source correctly | 34 | 15 | 32 | 13 |

Sources: [HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md). The difficulty split by round and the HS lab-name count were computed for this report from the pilot's [answer key](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/key/key-hs.json), [blind answers](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/check/blind-answers-hs.json) and [blind questions](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/blind/questions-hs.json), with their middle school counterparts.

### Accuracy held, but on easy questions checked by a sibling model

None of the 72 generated keys was wrong, which puts the true key-error rate below about **5% at 95% confidence**. All 31 generated calculations recomputed exactly, and all 16 randomly chosen keys matched their sources ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The DOE controls did worse. A 2007 high school question gives ultraviolet as the band in which supernovae are "most commonly detected", but the sources the checker found say supernovae are usually discovered by their visible light. A 2009 question leaves macrophages off a list of white blood cells, though OpenStax describes macrophages as monocytes that have left the blood ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md)). Two middle school controls had lesser problems: a gas-density bonus whose given density is impossible under the stated conditions, and a bird-flight question with a defensible second answer ([MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The pilot cannot claim the generated questions are more accurate. Zero wrong in 72 against 2 wrong in 36 is not statistically significant (one-sided Fisher's exact test, p ≈ 0.11). The generated questions were also easy textbook items, exactly where a writer and a checker from the same model family are most likely to agree.

Published research counsels caution. Across 71 medical studies, unscreened LLM drafts had error rates from under 1% to 45% ([Kıyak et al. 2026](https://academic.oup.com/pmj/advance-article/doi/10.1093/postmj/qgag057/8688271)). Expert-reviewed GPT-4 questions had more than one correct choice 4.9% of the time, against 1.1% for questions written by people ([Doughty et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03173)). And models are "much less accurate" when asked to write a question for a given numeric answer ([Balepur et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15512)). Human-written sets are not clean either: an audit of about 102,000 community Science Bowl questions found 2–3% wrong or unusable overall and 8% in math, physics and chemistry ([SB Reader](https://github.com/sbreader/sbreader.github.io)). The fair conclusion is that accuracy is under control at early-round difficulty but unproven for the hard questions Rebound most needs.

### Nearly half the first drafts restated a DOE question

Originality took up most of the pilot. When the writer checked its self-reviewed draft against all 23,720 library questions, it found that **33 of 72 (46%) asked for the same answer from the same cue as an existing DOE question**. That was 20 of 36 at middle school (56%) and 13 of 36 at high school ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)). A few were near-copies. A question on the shape of the xenon tetrafluoride molecule matched DOE's "down to the '[ZEE-non]' pronunciation guide", and a lens problem reused DOE's exact 10 cm and 20 cm. Most simply used the obvious cue for a basic fact, one DOE had already used: the outer core as the liquid layer, the new moon for a solar eclipse, argon as the third most abundant gas. Earth and Space lost 10 of its 12 question slots and Physics 8 of 12. The replacements fared little better. **22 of 59 replacement drafts (37%) also matched**, so **only 72 of the 131 questions written (55%) made it into the final rounds** (same source). Rebound's own duplicate check in its Write studio flags questions whose 64-bit SimHash fingerprints differ by nine bits or fewer. It **flagged none of the 41 matched pairs**, which were 13 to 41 bits apart, and comparing overlapping three-word sequences caught only 3 of the 41 ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md); [studio.ts](../src/core/studio.ts)).

The independent checker then found more. Three of the final middle school questions still asked for a DOE answer from DOE's cue: the steam turbine, the air trapped in down feathers and the partners in a lichen. A word-frequency similarity score (TF-IDF) rated all three below 0.6, so it would have missed them too ([MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The generation log shows that all three were themselves replacements written to fix earlier duplicates, as were six of the seven middle school matches the checker found ([generation log](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/logs/generation-meta.json)). A writer screening its own work keeps landing on the obvious cue. At high school, no final question copied a DOE answer and cue outright, but 9 of 36 reused a DOE calculation template with new numbers. One determinant question matched a DOE question word for word except for two numbers ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md)). This is known behavior of large language models. The more often a text is copied around the web, the more likely a model is to memorize it ([Carlini et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646)). GPT-4 filled in hidden answer choices from a public test word for word 57% of the time ([Deng et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783)), and different models write similar questions on narrow topics ([exam-security study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23729)). DOE's sets have been on the open web for years.

### Difficulty matched DOE's early rounds only at high school

On average the checkers rated the generated questions easier than the DOE controls: 1.94 against 2.50 at high school and 2.61 at middle school. But the controls came from every round, while the writer imitated a Round 1. Split by round, the high school questions were as hard as DOE's rounds 1–10 (1.94 against 1.91). The middle school questions fell short even of that tier (1.94 against 2.33), and neither level came near DOE's questions from round 11 on (3.43 and 3.17) ([blind answers](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/check/blind-answers-ms.json); [answer key](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/key/key-ms.json)). The samples are small, 6 to 12 controls per tier, and the ratings are one model's opinion. The writer's own estimates agree, though: its middle school round had 12 easy questions and no hard ones, and its replacements "leaned toward simple scenarios" ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)). Research predicts this drift. Experts judged 14% of GPT-4o exam questions to be at the wrong difficulty, against 1% of human-written ones, and the AI questions leaned toward recall ([Law et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-06796-6)). LLMs are also poor at judging how hard a question is from its text, especially a hard one ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28634)). Rebound cannot yet correct for this with data. Its mastery model says plainly that "question difficulty is not measured yet" and estimates difficulty from round number alone ([mastery.ts](../src/core/mastery.ts)).

### Style tells and an outdated lab name gave the source away

The checkers identified the source of **49 of 54** high school and **45 of 54** middle school questions, against about 56% for random guessing at the same rates. That overstates the difference, because most controls came from older sets with colon-ended questions and typos ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The real tells can be checked automatically. Generated choices ran longer, 3.5 words against DOE's 1.6 at middle school, and some carried "because" clauses. The correct choice was the single longest in 5 of 15 non-numeric middle school questions. Lab lead-ins sometimes dressed up a plain fact instead of introducing research. Bonuses ran long, with medians of 28 and 26 words against 19–26 and 15–21 in the DOE rounds the writer studied. Numbers were suspiciously round, and before self-review the first draft had put about half its multiple-choice answers on X (8 of 17 and 8 of 16) ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The generated questions also read aloud more cleanly than DOE's, with no typos, NOT-questions or colon endings.

Freshness failed in a way no blind solver could see. The middle school checker found that the **National Renewable Energy Laboratory was renamed the National Laboratory of the Rockies on 1 December 2025** ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/eere/articles/energy-department-renames-nrel-national-lab-rockies)). The old name also appears in three high school Energy questions that the high school checker passed, so **5 of the 12 generated Energy questions use an outdated lab name** ([blind questions](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot/blind/questions-hs.json)). None of the writer's 11 claims about lab research was checked against a source ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)). The pilot also shows which checks earn their keep. The blind solve found no error in any generated key; its only disagreements exposed DOE's errors. The problems that mattered (duplicates, level, giveaways and the outdated name) turned up through library search, a web check and style comparison. The checkers' own flags were noisy: the middle school checker flagged 29 of 54 questions and withdrew 18 of those flags after checking ([MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)). The machine effort was small. The writer spent 46 minutes of one agent's time on the whole run, about four of them drafting all 72 questions and most of the rest on originality ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)).

## Original questions are legally clean; official branding is not

### DOE's samples rest on agency policy, not on the copyright statute

DOE grants no license for its sample questions and sets no restriction. The sample pages carry no copyright or terms text, and none of the 509 PDFs carries a notice ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md)). The governing text is the Office of Science web policy. It says **"Materials on the Office of Science website are in the public domain"** and asks to be credited as the source. It also asks users to link to its pages rather than download portions of them, forbids using its materials to imply endorsement, and bars DOE or Office of Science logos without permission ([Office of Science Web Policies](https://science.osti.gov/Web-Policies)). The legal basis is narrower than Rebound's own wording suggests. The relevant copyright statute, 17 U.S.C. §105, bars copyright only in works "prepared by an officer or employee of the United States Government" ([17 U.S.C. §105, via search excerpt](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2020-title17/html/USCODE-2020-title17-chap1-sec105.htm)). Its legislative history leaves it to each agency to decide whether a contractor may claim copyright in work the government paid for ([§105 notes, via search excerpt](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-134/t17/s105/)). Contractors are documented as NSB's question writers in 2005, 2009 and 2026, so the questions' public-domain status rests on DOE's published policy, not on §105.

Rebound's README, Info page and NSB-JSON example all call the sets "U.S. Government works" ([README](../README.md); [Info.tsx](../src/pages/Info.tsx); [NSB-JSON.md](../docs/NSB-JSON.md)). Rebound already credits DOE and links every Library round to its original PDF (same sources), so the practical risk of continuing to reuse the sets is low. The license line should still cite DOE's policy rather than the statute. Rule 1-2 is the one hard limit: players may see only official questions "published on the NSB website" ([2027 Rules](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)). Rebound must therefore never take in unreleased official questions, including any recalled by people who signed DOE's nondisclosure form.

### The NSB name is a registered mark, and descriptive use is fine

NATIONAL SCIENCE BOWL is a live DOE service mark (Reg. No. 4082475, renewed 2022). It is registered with "SCIENCE BOWL" disclaimed, meaning DOE claims no exclusive right to those two words alone, and the NSB logo is registered separately ([USPTO TSDR](https://tsdr.uspto.gov/statusview/sn85226166); [USPTO TSDR, logo](https://tsdr.uspto.gov/statusview/sn86025872)). DOE's logo policy bars any use that "falsely implies employment by, or affiliation with" the NSB, as well as any commercial use of the logo ([NSB Logos](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/About/Logos)). Naming the competition that Rebound helps players practice for is ordinary descriptive use, and Rebound already states that it is "not affiliated with or endorsed by" DOE ([README](../README.md)). Generated sets should therefore be called Rebound practice rounds in NSB format, never "National Science Bowl questions", and should carry no DOE or NSB logo. DOE actively encourages original questions. Its FAQ says "You can also write your own", its tips page calls students writing their own practice questions "a good practice", and it lists outside resources with only a not-endorsed disclaimer ([NSB FAQ](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/FAQ); [Tips and Resources](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Tips-and-Resources); [Outside Resources](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Outside-Resources-and-Links.pdf)). A text search of the 2027 rules, the virtual rules, the 2026 manual and the FAQ found no mention of AI at all ([NSB production analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/nsb_question_production.md)).

### AI-written questions can be published but not owned

AI authorship changes who owns the questions, not whether Rebound may publish them. The U.S. Copyright Office holds that "copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material" and that "prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control" ([USCO Part 2](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)). The D.C. Circuit upheld the requirement of a human author in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, and the Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal on 2 March 2026 ([D.C. Cir. opinion](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-final-word-supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-case-on-ai-authorship)). So Rebound may publish generated questions freely but cannot stop others from copying them. Only human edits, and the creative selection and arrangement of a set, carry thin protection. Releasing generated sets under CC0 or CC BY would make that explicit for teams. Under Anthropic's Commercial Terms, the customer owns the outputs, and Anthropic assigns whatever rights it has in them, "if any". Its promise to defend customers against intellectual-property claims excludes outputs the customer has modified or combined with other content, so it is no insurance for edited, source-based question sets ([Anthropic Commercial Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms)).

### Inputs, not outputs, carry the licensing risk

What goes into the prompts needs the most care. NAQT now forbids putting its content into any AI model without written permission ([NAQT 2026–27 changes, via search summary](https://www.naqt.com/articles/2026-2027-changes.jsp)). Invitational and school-team packets carry no open license ([SB Reader](https://github.com/sbreader/sbreader.github.io)). Sources under CC BY-SA or noncommercial licenses impose their terms only when a question copies their wording rather than their facts ([CC AI primer](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/); [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en)). Public-domain material from U.S. agencies is the safest source to work from, with two cautions. NASA forbids attributing AI-generated content to NASA ([NASA guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)), and DOE notes that material contributed to its sites by private parties can be copyrighted ([DOE Web Policies](https://www.energy.gov/about-us/web-policies)).

Community opinion points the same way as the law. Quiz bowl forums are hostile to AI-written questions, mainly for paid tournaments and on grounds of authenticity, and one league's writing guide bans AI outright ([hsquizbowl essay thread, via search summary](https://hsquizbowl.org/forums/viewtopic.php?t=28977); [LIQBA, via search summary](https://liqba.com/writing-questions/)). A free, clearly labeled practice tool sidesteps the "I want my money back" objection, but not the accuracy one. Rebound's current rule is that AI-made content "stays in the team's practice", and Write studio packets carry an "AI-drafted (internal practice only)" note ([README](../README.md); [studio.ts](../src/core/studio.ts)). A public library of generated sets is therefore a policy decision as much as a technical one.

## Dollars of compute, hours of review: the recommended pipeline

### Compute costs dollars a year; human review is the real budget

Rebound's cost model prices a four-step pipeline: writing with a cached style guide, a blind solve, settling disagreements, and code checks of numeric answers. It uses Claude API prices from cached documentation that was not checked against the live price list ([Rebound cost model](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). For mixes that use Sonnet or Opus, it comes to **about $11–$45 per 1,000 generated questions at standard rates and $6–$23 through the half-price batch interface**, or $0.28–$2.27 per 50-question round. Running every step on Haiku would cost about $1 per 1,000. The model's output and reasoning tokens make up 85–90% of the cost. This report adjusted those figures for what the pilot found: it applied the pilot's measured yield (55% of drafts kept) and added one model call per question to judge originality. On that basis, three accepted rounds a week, or 7,800 questions a year, cost **roughly $110–$810 a year** across those mixes. The pilot did not record its own token use, so this is an estimate. Human writing costs far more. At NAQT's 2026–27 maximum base rates of $10.06 per toss-up and $7.78 per bonus, writer pay alone for a 23-pair round is about $410, or about $70,000 a year at the same volume ([NAQT jobs, via search summary](https://www.naqt.com/jobs/)). Science Bowl questions are shorter than quiz bowl questions, so the true human cost is lower, but not by enough to close the gap. The real budget is reviewer time, which the pilot did not measure. The nearest evidence comes from medical exams: drafting with AI plus expert revision took 24.5 person-hours per 100 questions, against 96 to write them by hand ([Law et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-06796-6)).

The pipeline should be built around the failures the pilot found, in this order:

| Stage | What it does | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Plan the round | Fill a round template: 23 pairs (in person) or 18 (virtual), the fixed category order, 25–40% multiple choice, and difficulty set by round position. Aim at topics the library covers thinly | The obvious cues for famous facts are taken. Thin topics such as high school nuclear chemistry, equilibrium and reaction rates, and middle school chemistry, still have room ([Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)) |
| 2. Ground each question | Build Energy questions from dated DOE lab news, with its link and the lab's current name. Other questions cite a public-domain or CC BY source | 5 of 12 Energy questions used a renamed lab. The Write studio already requires a cited source ([MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md); [studio.ts](../src/core/studio.ts)) |
| 3. Steer the writer away from DOE's cues | Show the writer the library's existing questions for each target answer as cues to avoid. Ask for applied, scenario-based and multi-step questions | 46% of drafts and 37% of replacements matched DOE ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)) |
| 4. Run the free code checks | Run the studio's `checkWritten` checks. Run the answer checker on the key, every ACCEPT line and every wrong choice. Shuffle unordered choices. Flag long choices, a correct choice that is the longest too often, "because" clauses, and answer words or lab names in the question | Bunched keys, giveaways and length clues were the most common problems ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)) |
| 5. Compute numbers in code | Recompute every numeric answer by running code | Having a model write and run a program beats its own arithmetic ([PAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10435)). Writing a question for a numeric answer is where LLMs are weakest ([Balepur et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15512)) |
| 6. Independent blind solve | A stronger model, or one from a different family, answers without the key and says whether the question is well-posed. Disagreements are settled or the question is dropped | This step caught DOE's wrong keys. Agreement between sibling models is weak evidence, and models answer flawed questions instead of flagging them ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md); [SciFaultyQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11988)) |
| 7. Screen for duplicates by answer and cue | Retrieve library, team-packet and earlier generated questions with the same answer. A model judges whether the cue is the same. Cap reused calculation templates per round | SimHash caught 0 of 41 matches, and TF-IDF missed all three duplicates that survived ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md); [MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)) |
| 8. Assemble the rounds | Enforce topic variety within each category, the format mix, a spread of difficulty and limits on bonus length | Three of six high school Energy questions were about neutrons, and each passed review on its own ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md)) |
| 9. Human sign-off | A subject owner approves each question, concentrating on hard, ambiguous and Energy questions | The problems that mattered needed judgment and sources ([MS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-ms.md)) |
| 10. Label and track | Record the model, date, sources and blind result in the schema's existing `ai` field. Release under CC0 or CC BY. Add a "report this question" button, and retire questions that perform oddly in play | The schema already has `blindAnswer` and `blindAgreed` fields ([schema.ts](../src/core/schema.ts)) |

### Rebound already has most of the plumbing

Fitting generated sets into Rebound needs little new machinery. The schema already has a `rebound` source type for questions "the site generates itself". The site already generates questions too: 39 visual-bonus templates draw figures and compute their answers exactly ([schema.ts](../src/core/schema.ts); [Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). Rebound tracks which questions each player has heard by question id, so a new `rebound-hs-…` set with stable ids counts as fresh in drills, plans and scrimmages, with no code change, once it is in the catalog (same analysis). There are two ways to deliver it. **Team packets** work today. The pilot's NSB-JSON files pass Rebound's real upload code with no warnings, but packets stay private to one team, display as text with no recorded voice, and hold about 1,100 questions per stored record ([pilot generation note](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-generation.md); [Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). **The public library** needs four changes: a build step that writes the set, index, catalog and concept files without a source PDF; a relaxed version of the test that requires every set id to start with `doe-hs-` or `doe-ms-` ([doe-library.test.ts](../src/core/importers/doe-library.test.ts)); regenerated duplicate fingerprints; and a voice recording of every question, made with Kokoro on the captain's Windows PC, because a test blocks deployment until every question has one ([Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). The machine time for voice is trivial: about 12 seconds to synthesize a 50-question round and 20 seconds to check it with Whisper. The free Cloudflare Workers that host the recordings have room for about 16,400 more, roughly two years at three rounds a week (same analysis). Difficulty enters only through the catalog's round number, so each generated question needs a deliberately chosen nominal round. Two small mismatches need fixing first. The studio's packet builder defaults to 25 pairs per round, a size DOE last used in 2013, and it hard-codes the high school level ([studio.ts](../src/core/studio.ts)).

### Audit first, team packets next, public library last

Generated sets should earn their way into the public library in three steps.

**First, point the checking pipeline at the existing library.** Blind-solving all 23,720 DOE questions would cost about $80–$320 at the modeled rates, before settling disagreements ([Rebound cost model](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)). The pilot found wrong keys in 2 of 36 DOE controls (a 95% confidence interval of 0.7–19%), though a model that has memorized DOE's keys would miss some of these errors ([HS verification](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/pilot-verification-hs.md)). The license wording and a labeling standard can be fixed at the same time.

**Second, run a season through team packets**, at the volume subject owners can genuinely review; one round per level per week is a sensible start. Compare how often players answer generated questions correctly on first hearing with how often the same players answer DOE questions correctly. Per-player records already allow this within a team ([Rebound library analysis](../research_notes/Making%20Science%20Bowl%20questions%20at%20scale/rebound_library_and_costs.md)).

**Third, move generated sets into the public library** only once they perform like DOE questions at their assigned round and error reports stay low. Then scale toward three rounds a week, the rate that would offset the drain on fresh scrimmage material. Two additions would let difficulty be measured from real play instead of guessed: an opt-in way to combine results across accounts, and a record of how far into a question each early buzz came (same analysis).

## Conclusion

The pilot turns the usual worry about AI-written questions upside down. At early-round difficulty, generated keys were wrong no more often than DOE's own. The binding constraint is originality, and it is built into the problem: better prompts can steer around it but cannot remove it. The 23,720 published questions already use the obvious cue for most famous facts, and every set Rebound adds leaves less room. To escape, the writer must move toward applied questions, multi-step calculations, current lab research and hard late-round material, which is exactly where LLM question writers fail most. Checking will therefore matter more as the pipeline matures, not less. A test passed on easy recall says little about the round-17 questions Rebound most needs, so the next pilot should target those on purpose, with a checker from outside the writer's model family.

The same evidence changes how Rebound should treat the library it inherited. By DOE's own account, those 23,720 questions are material to audit, not an answer key to trust, and the checker built for generated questions can clean them for a few hundred dollars. The research found no one else offering a verified, renewable supply of original NSB-format practice questions. Rebound can build one on clean legal ground, provided it labels the questions plainly, never presents them as official, and lets real players' answers, not a model's judgment, decide how hard they are.
