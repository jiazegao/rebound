## 1. Who writes and reviews NSB questions (people, recruitment, pay, scale, named leads)

### Takeaway
DOE does not publicly name its question writers. The best current evidence, a May 2026 release from ORISE (DOE's contractor, run by ORAU), says ORISE staff developed the questions for the 2026 competition. Earlier sources point to an outside company: Avery Enterprises in 2005, and a 2009 DOE FAQ saying DOE hires "a company" through government contracting. An older DOE regional fact sheet instead says scientists at DOE facilities, other agencies and universities submitted questions. Review is by unnamed "NSB subject-matter experts", and the only named official is the DOE National Coordinator, Jan Tyler. Volunteers read, judge and check questions at regionals, but they are not described as the writers.

### Inferences
- Over time, writing has moved through at least three arrangements:
  - scientist submissions, an undated early model;
  - a contracted question company (2005–2009);
  - ORISE staff developing questions, confirmed for 2026. When ORISE took over writing is unknown.
- ORISE probably runs the work as a managed project that draws in subject-matter experts, not with an open volunteer pool. This rests on two things: the DOE manual's references to "NSB subject-matter experts", and ORISE's general role of finding experts for DOE reviews (see section 7).
- Writers are probably paid: either contractor staff, or experts paid through the contractor. Regional volunteers are unpaid.
- The number of writers and reviewers is not published.
  - The 2005 posting (several freelancers for 4,000+ questions) and the "at least two" subject-expert reviewers per subject (section 2) suggest a core team in the low tens.
  - On top of that come hundreds of regional volunteers, who serve as readers and checkers, not writers. This is an inference only.
- For Rebound's pipeline, NSB's own model looks like this: a small paid central writing team working from standard textbooks (section 3), at least two subject experts reviewing each subject, a single national editor or coordinator who collects errata, and distributed local expert review before play.

### Gaps
- No current DOE or ORISE document names the writers, how they are recruited, what they are paid, or how many there are. DOE does not publish this.
- The ORISE 2026 and 2022 releases, the 2009 FAQ, the 2005 Avery posts and the Nevada sheets could not be opened directly, so their wording is from search excerpts.
- It is unknown whether former competitors, teachers (for example the Einstein Fellows, whom DOE funds alongside NSB) or national-lab staff currently write official questions. A search for an Einstein Fellow reviewing NSB questions was not confirmed.
- No separate federal contract for NSB question writing was found (see section 6, USAspending). This fits question development being funded inside the ORISE prime contract, but that could not be confirmed.

## 2. What review, editing and vetting steps questions go through

### Takeaway
DOE describes a central review followed by local review, with an errata loop:
- Each subject's questions are reviewed by "at least two individuals with subject expertise", then sent to coordinators.
- Coordinators are urged to have local experts review the packets before game day.
- Errata go to the National Coordinator, who sends them to the NSB subject-matter experts. Approved changes are circulated to all regionals.
- DOE itself says errors still turn up "almost every week".

No documentation was found of formal fact-checking, statistical difficulty calibration or playtesting.

### Cited Findings
- **Two subject experts per question.** "The questions in each subject area have been reviewed by at least two individuals with subject expertise to eliminate erroneous or ambiguous questions." DOE adds that it uses multiple choice for many questions, which "ensures against there being more than one correct answer." — [2026 Coordinator Manual, "The Question Judge"](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf). The same sentence appears in the [2020 manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/coordinator-resources/NSB_CoordManual.pdf?la=en&hash=A5A2CB04209FF8488FBF7412E7E22363D89CBAF6).
- **Errors still slip through.** "Although the questions are reviewed by a group of science/math experts prior to being sent to the regional coordinators, we continue to find errors, omitted words, and formatting issues almost every week. As a result, each regional site is encouraged to have a team of experts review the questions before the event. Please send feedback/errata from the review to jan.tyler@science.doe.gov for review and possible circulation to other regional events." — [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
  - The 2020 wording lacked the "errors… almost every week" admission: "The questions are reviewed by a group of science/math experts prior to being sent… Please send feedback/errata… for circulation to other regionals." — [2020 manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/coordinator-resources/NSB_CoordManual.pdf?la=en&hash=A5A2CB04209FF8488FBF7412E7E22363D89CBAF6)
- **The errata loop.** Coordinators should "Try to find experts in every subject area to review the questions BEFORE Game Day and send their comments to jan.tyler@science.doe.gov".
  - "The National Coordinator will send all comments to the NSB subject-matter experts to determine if a change should be made."
  - "The National Coordinator will then share approved changes with all of the regional coordinators."
  - Coordinators can also use "the NSB Science Bowl Central Zoom Room to discuss challenges and other issues that arise."
  - Source: [2026 Coordinator Manual, Tips](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
- **Local review roles.** Veteran coordinators recommend that the regional committee include "Question Review" and someone "responsible for the accuracy of questions". The timeline puts "Review competition questions" one month before the event. — [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
  - In 2020 the wording was: find experts to "(a) check over and amend the questions BEFORE Game Day and (b) be available ON Game Day for appeals." — [2020 manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/coordinator-resources/NSB_CoordManual.pdf?la=en&hash=A5A2CB04209FF8488FBF7412E7E22363D89CBAF6)
- **Reading checks.** The Moderator "will be provided with the questions well in advance… It is important that the Moderator read all the questions before the competition."
  - Moderators and Question Judges "MUST be able to clearly enunciate the questions and properly pronounce the scientific terms."
  - Some regionals hold moderator auditions.
  - Source: [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
  - In play, the Question Judge follows an identical packet to make sure the moderator reads "from the correct round" and "correctly (not skipping words, correct pronunciations)." — [Question Judge one-pager (2023 PDF)](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/volunteer-training/questionjudge.pdf)
- **Difficulty is set by round position, not by stated calibration.**
  - "Questions are written to match the grade level of the students and become progressively more difficult in later competition rounds."
  - "Questions in Rounds 1 - 10 are fairly equal in difficulty. Beginning with Round 11, questions become more difficult by round, with Round 17 being the most difficult."
  - Source: [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
  - The FAQ says HS questions are "at a college freshman level." — [NSB FAQ](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/FAQ)
- **Source material.** "National Science Bowl questions are by and large sourced from science textbooks commonly used…". DOE lists sample textbooks by level, such as Campbell *Biology*, Giancoli *Physics*, Zumdahl *Chemistry* and Seeds *Foundations of Astronomy*. It also lists MIT OpenCourseWare and DOE national laboratory websites, and calls the list "not binding". — [Tips and Resources](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Tips-and-Resources)
- **Answer conventions.** The rules include an appendix of question and answer conventions. Examples: numbers are base 10, geometry is Euclidean, trig arguments are in radians, dice are fair and six-sided, and numerical answers must be in exact, simplest form. — [2027 Rules, Appendix (dated 10-04-2026)](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf). Format details are covered by another researcher.
- **Errors remain in published sets.** Rebound's import of the published sets found and flagged printed errors:
  - misprinted option letters;
  - answer letters that contradict the answer text;
  - "Mutliple Choice" and "ANWER:" typos;
  - a bonus printed under a second TOSS-UP marker;
  - DOE's own note in MS Set 3 Round 7: "The toss-up and bonus for Question 8 have been omitted."

### Inferences
- NSB's quality control relies on many people checking each packet: two central experts, then dozens of regional expert teams reading the same set before and during play, with errata pushed nationally. Rebound has no army of live readers. An LLM pipeline would need to replace that downstream net with automated checks plus human review before release.
- Whether an answer is correct is judged at a single point in time. DOE's own disclaimer ("answers to some of the questions change") implies no systematic re-verification of old items. A generator should date-stamp facts and re-check time-sensitive items.
- Multiple choice is used deliberately to remove ambiguity. That design choice can be copied by generators to cut challenge risk.

### Gaps
- DOE does not describe:
  - an editorial (style) pass separate from subject review;
  - formal fact-checking against references;
  - pre-testing or playtesting with students;
  - item statistics used to calibrate difficulty.
- The identities, number and selection of the "NSB subject-matter experts" are not published.
- No public errata log exists for live seasons or for the published sample sets.

## 3. How rounds and sets are assembled, and how many questions a season needs

### Takeaway
DOE makes several sets of 17-round packets each year. Regionals get one set based on location and event date, and rounds 1–10 (round robin) are easier than rounds 11–17 (elimination). A round alternates toss-up and bonus pairs across a fixed category mix. In 2014–2020 that meant 23 pairs per round: four in each of five categories and three in Energy. Since 2021–22 the six categories have been equal. The National Finals use their own rounds, including visual bonuses, and tiebreakers are five-toss-up sets. DOE does not publish a season total. A lower-bound estimate is roughly 3,000–5,000 new questions per season.

### Cited Findings
- **Multiple sets.** "There are multiple sets of questions used in the regional and national competitions. Depending on the location and event date, regional coordinators will receive one of the sets of questions in early January. The goal is to limit the chances that a visitor might overhear a question that they share with competitors in future competitions."
  - "Round Robin competitions should use Rounds 1 - 10 and use the later rounds for the elimination tournaments."
  - Source: [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
- **Regional scale.** In FY 2025, more than 3,500 MS students (448 schools) and 6,400 HS students (846 schools) took part in 115 regional competitions. The April 2025 Finals had 47 MS and 68 HS teams at the National Conference Center, Leesburg, Virginia. — [FY 2027 WDTS budget narrative](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/budget/pdf/sc-budget-request-to-congress/2027/FY-2027-Request-Narrative---WDTS.pdf)
  - The manual cites "65 high school and 50 middle school regional Science Bowl tournaments." — [2026 Coordinator Manual](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/NSB-Coordinator-Manual.pdf)
  - The 2026 Finals (April 30–May 4, 2026) fielded 68 HS and 48 MS regional champions. — [DOE press release, May 4, 2026](https://www.energy.gov/science/articles/students-california-and-massachusetts-win-does-36th-annual-national-science-bowlr)
- **Categories (current).** "The question categories for both middle school and high school are Biology, Chemistry, Earth and Space Science, Energy, Mathematics, and Physics." — [2027 Rules, Rule 3-1](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)
  - The FAQ is stale and contradicts itself. It says HS questions "cover seven categories" but lists six, and gives the MS categories as "life science, physical science, earth and space science, energy, mathematics." — [NSB FAQ](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/FAQ)
- **Category mix per round (DOE's published sets).**
  - In the 17-round HS sets of 2015–2020, each of Biology, Chemistry, Earth and Space, Math and Physics totals about 136 questions per set, and Energy totals 102. That is 782 questions, or 23 toss-up/bonus pairs per round, split 4-4-4-4-4-3 with Energy getting 3.

  - The 25-pair sets up to 2013 also give Energy about 102 per 17 rounds.

  - 25 toss-ups and 25 bonuses per round through 2013 (and in the MS Sample Rounds).

  - Current regional play: "two 8-minute halves with a 2-minute break." — [2027 Rules, Rule 5-1](https://science.osti.gov/-/media/wdts/nsb/pdf/NSB-Resources/Rules2027.pdf)

- **Reuse across years and levels.**

  - In the published sets, MS 2022 Round 7 contains the same 36 questions as HS 2022 Round 10. Fifty-seven question texts recur within the MS library, and 24 MS questions also appear in the HS library. — /home/user/rebound/scripts/doe/REPORT-MS.md, from the [MS Sample Questions](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/MS-Sample-Questions)
- **Historical volume.** DOE's invitational page says the public practice bank "grows by 32 rounds each year." — [Invitational Practice Tournament (2019)](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/nsb/Regional-Competitions/Resources/Invitational-Practice-Tournament). The 2005 writer call covered 4,000+ questions for one season. — [hsquizbowl (via search summary)](https://www.hsquizbowl.org/forums/viewtopic.php?t=1268)

### Inferences
- A rough lower bound per season, for planning only:
  - One 17-round regional set is 782 questions (23 toss-ups and 23 bonuses per round).
  - With at least two regional sets per level, two levels need at least 3,128 regional questions.
  - On top of that come the Finals rounds (round robin, double elimination, visual bonuses), tiebreakers and spare rounds.
  - That makes about 3,500–5,000 questions per season. This is consistent with the 2005 "4,000+" figure, but it is an inference, not a DOE number.
- Rebound's 23,720-question library is about five to seven seasons of DOE output. A renewable generator matching NSB's mix would produce 46-question rounds (23+23 pairs) in the 2014–2020 style, or 36-question rounds in the 2021–22 equal-share style, with category quotas per round.
- The same questions appear at more than one level, so DOE's level-tuning is partly "reuse plus selection", not wholly separate writing for MS and HS.

### Gaps
- DOE does not publish:
  - how many sets are written per season or per level;
  - how National Finals rounds are sized (number of toss-ups per round at Finals) beyond halves and visual-bonus timing;
  - how categories are ordered within a round (no rotation rule is stated);
  - the size of the tiebreaker pool.
- The national packet structure (round robin plus double elimination, number of rounds) needs the Finals schedule. That is the other researcher's format area.

## 4. Timeline, delivery and security; how errors are handled during play

### Takeaway
Questions reach coordinators in early January for regionals held January–March. Moderators and judges get them in advance. Everyone with access is supposed to complete a nondisclosure and conflict-of-interest form, and packets are collected and destroyed after play. DOE stopped requiring paper-only packets after 2020, and now lets them be "computer-based". During play, only the four active players may challenge, within strict limits. Challenges go to subject experts at a central appeals desk, and "All decisions of the judges are final." Pre-event errata are routed nationally.

### Cited Findings

## 6. Terms of use: copyright, 17 U.S.C. §105, trademark, branding, third-party resources, team-written questions

### Takeaway
- **Copyright.** No copyright or usage notice appears on the sample pages or inside any sample PDF. The Office of Science's site-wide policy says its materials "are in the public domain" and asks for credit.
  - Strictly, §105 covers only works by federal employees. Questions developed by contractors (Avery in 2005; ORISE in 2026) are not automatically federal works.
  - DOE presents them uncopyrighted, so practical risk is low but not zero.
- **Trademark.** "NATIONAL SCIENCE BOWL" is a live DOE registration, with "SCIENCE BOWL" disclaimed. The NSB logo is also registered.
- **DOE's conditions.** No commercial use of the logo, no implied affiliation or endorsement, and DOE/SC logos only with permission.
- **Third-party resources.** DOE lists them with a "not officially endorsed" disclaimer, and it encourages teams to write and swap their own questions.

## 7. History and recent changes (categories, round structure, 2023–2026)

### Takeaway
The category changes:
- Computer Science was dropped around 2002.
- About 2010–2011, Astronomy and Earth Science merged into Earth and Space Science, Energy was added, and HS General Science was dropped.
- MS dropped General Science around 2017.
- MS moved to the HS six-category scheme by the 2022 season. The 2027 rules now give both levels the same six categories.

Round lengths shrank from 25 to 23 questions per round in 2014, and to 15–18 in the 2021–22 virtual seasons. Recent changes include virtual regionals, non-verbal toss-up communication (2025) and visual bonuses at the Finals. There is still no AI rule. Meanwhile DOE's FY2027 request nearly doubles the NSB budget line to fund a national AI Challenge, and the ORISE contract, which covers question development, is being re-competed for October 2027.

### Inferences
- Energy, introduced around 2010–11, carries DOE's mission content. It was under-weighted until 2020 (3 of 23 pairs per round) and has been equal since 2021–22. Generated sets should follow the current equal six-way split and weave in DOE-lab and energy-research contexts, as the official Energy questions do.
- MS questions now use HS category names (Biology, Chemistry and Physics instead of Life and Physical Science). This happened by 2022 in the published sets and is formalized in the 2027 rules, so an MS generator should target the HS taxonomy at MS difficulty. The NSB FAQ has not caught up.
- No AI rule covers question writing or use, so nothing in DOE's published rules bars Rebound from AI-generating practice questions. The real constraints are accuracy and the trademark and endorsement limits above.
- The budget increase is for an AI Challenge, not for question writing. That means no sign of more released questions.
- The ORISE re-competition could change who develops NSB questions from October 2027. That could change question style or release practice, and is worth watching.

### Gaps
- DOE publishes no official change log for categories or round formats. The dates for CS (2002) and for the Astronomy/General Science/Energy change (2010–11) rest on Wikipedia, school pages and DOE's sample sets, not a DOE announcement.
- The start year of the middle school competition is unconfirmed. The evidence is MS PDFs headed "Junior National Science Bowl" (2004), and archived "/nmsb/" pages (National Middle School Science Bowl) seen only in search listings.
- The exact season when MS switched categories (2022 sample set compared with the official rules of 2022–2026) could not be confirmed: the 2024–2026 rules PDFs now return 404 or were blocked, and the Wayback Machine was unavailable.
- No 2023–2026 DOE statement was found on how questions are written, on the use of AI by question writers, or on whether DOE plans more frequent releases.
